Part 1: BO vs Grid Search vs Random Search (BO_comp.png)
-------------------------------------------------------------

Looking at the BO_comp.png plot, we compared EI, LCB, Random Search and 
Grid Search over 20 function evaluations on the Levy 1D function.

The first thing that stands out is how much better both BO methods (EI and 
LCB) perform compared to the baselines. By around evaluation 5-7, EI and LCB 
already start finding much better values than random and grid search manage 
to find even after all 20 evaluations.

LCB seems to be the most aggressive optimizer - it drops to around 1e-5 by 
the end, which is the lowest of all methods. EI is close behind reaching 
around 1e-3. Both clearly outperform the baselines.

Random search does surprisingly okay - it manages to find decent solutions 
eventually but is very inconsistent (the shaded variance region is huge). 
This makes sense because it gets lucky sometimes but has no strategy.

Grid search performs the worst overall. It stays high (around 1e-2 to 1e-1) 
for most of the run and barely improves. This is because with only 20 
evaluations in [-15, 10], the grid points are spaced too far apart to find 
the minimum at x=1.

The confidence bands (shaded areas) for EI and LCB are also much tighter 
than for random and grid search, meaning BO is more consistent across seeds. 
Random search has massive variance - sometimes it gets lucky, sometimes not.

Key takeaway: With a very limited budget of 20 evaluations, BO clearly wins. 
It uses past observations to guide where to search next, which is critical 
when evaluations are expensive. Grid and random search waste evaluations in 
regions that are clearly bad.

BO.png - GP Model and Acquisition Functions
-------------------------------------------------------------

The top plot shows the GP surrogate model fitted on 10 random observations 
of the Levy function. The model (green) tracks the true function (blue) well 
near the observed points but the uncertainty (orange band) grows large in 
the region around x > 2 where there's little data. This is exactly what 
we'd expect from a GP - it's confident near observations and uncertain far 
from them.

LCB (orange) in the top plot overlaps almost perfectly with the model mean 
near x=0 to x=2 and shows wider bounds further right, encouraging 
exploration in that uncertain region.

The EI plot (bottom) peaks around x=3 to x=6, which makes sense - this is 
where the GP thinks there might be good values (low mean) AND there's high 
uncertainty. The EI being near 0 on the left side means the model is 
confident those regions are not worth exploring since we already have 
observations showing high function values there.

Part 2: NePS Trajectory (NEPS-trajectory.png)
-------------------------------------------------------------

Adding NEPS to the comparison is interesting. NEPS (purple) drops quickly 
in the first 3 evaluations to around 1e-1 but then completely flatlines - 
it doesn't improve at all for the remaining 17 evaluations. This is 
surprising and suggests either:
- NEPS got stuck in a local minimum
- The budget of 20 evaluations was too small for NEPS to properly explore 
  and then exploit (it needs more initial random points)
- NEPS might need more evaluations to "warm up" before its BO kicks in

EI and LCB still clearly outperform everything. LCB again reaches the 
lowest values (~1e-5) by evaluation 20, showing it's the most 
sample-efficient on this 1D function.

Random search continues to be erratic but manages around 1e-1 to 1e-2 
which is better than NEPS in this budget regime, which is a bit 
unexpected.

Grid search performs poorly as before, stuck around 1e-2 to 1e-1.

Effect of budget: With 20 evaluations, model-based methods like EI and LCB 
shine because they learn from every single observation. Increasing the budget 
would likely help NEPS more than EI/LCB since NEPS probably needs more 
exploration before it can effectively exploit. Grid search would also improve 
more with budget since it just needs more points to cover the space.

Summary
-------------------------------------------------------------

Best performer:   LCB (most sample efficient, lowest final value)
Second:           EI  (consistent, good across seeds)
Third:            Random Search (erratic but occasionally lucky)
Fourth:           NEPS (plateaus too early with small budget)
Worst:            Grid Search (wastes budget on bad regions)

Overall, model-based BO methods are clearly worth the extra complexity when
function evaluations are expensive and the budget is small. The difference
between BO and baselines is most dramatic in the 5-15 evaluation range.

Part 3: HPOSuite BO Comparison (hposuite-bo-comparison.png)
-------------------------------------------------------------

We compared four SoTA BO optimizers on the BBOB Schwefel 5D function
(f20, instance 0) with a budget of 20 evaluations and 3 seeds:
SMAC_BO, Optuna (TPE), Scikit-Optimize (GP+EI), and NepsBO.

Final ranking at eval 20 (lower is better):
  1st: SMAC_BO        (~1,500  - near zero)
  2nd: Optuna         (~8,000)
  3rd: Scikit_Optimize (~16,000)
  4th: NepsBO         (~19,000)

Performance/Behavior Differences:

SMAC_BO  performs the best overall. It starts around 26,000 and stays 
flat until about evaluation 10, after which it drops sharply to near 
zero and remains there. This suggests that the Random Forest surrogate 
becomes reliable around that point and is able to guide the search 
effectively toward a good region. Its performance also becomes very 
consistent toward the end, as seen by the reduced variance across runs. 
This method handles the multimodal Schwefel function well.

Optuna shows the most stable and gradual improvement. It steadily decreases 
from around 40,000 to about 8,000 over all 20 evaluations, without any clear 
plateau. This indicates consistent progress throughout the budget. Its TPE-based 
approach works well in multimodal settings, and the variance remains moderate, 
showing fairly consistent behavior across different runs.

Scikit-Optimize GP+EI improves quickly in the first few evaluations (from 
~28,000 to ~18,000), but then stops improving and plateaus for the rest of the
 budget. This suggests that it gets stuck in a local minimum early on. The GP model 
 assumes smoothness, which does not match the highly multimodal nature of the
  Schwefel function, making it difficult to explore effectively.

NepsBO has the weakest performance initially, starting around 65,000 with very 
high variance. It remains almost flat until about evaluation 17, after which it 
improves to around 19,000. This indicates that a large portion of its budget is 
spent on initial exploration, leaving limited time for effective optimization. 
The high variance also shows inconsistent behavior across runs.

Variance: All methods show high variance, especially in the early evaluations. 
This suggests that a budget of 20 evaluations is too small for reliable optimization 
in a 5D problem. Early results are strongly influenced by random initialization, 
which leads to differences between runs.

Effect of Budget:
With a small budget (20 evaluations), SMAC_BO performs best because it is able to 
build a useful model relatively quickly.
With larger budgets (50–100 evaluations):
 - Optuna is expected to improve further and may approach or match SMAC_BO.
 - GP-based methods (Scikit-Optimize and NepsBO) would benefit from more data, as their 
   models become more accurate over time.
 - NepsBO would also improve since its initial exploration phase would take up a 
   smaller portion of the total budget.
